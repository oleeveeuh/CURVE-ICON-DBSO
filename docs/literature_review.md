# Literature Review: ECoG-Based Motor Decoding and Kinematic Biomarkers in Parkinson's Disease

## Scope and relationship to this project

This review evaluates recent work relevant to the active CURVE-ICON-DBSO question: whether intracranial cortical electrophysiology contains information about finger-tapping performance in people with Parkinson's disease (PD). The current project uses approximately 31 intraoperative recordings, resting and movement ECoG blocks, synchronized sensor-glove signals, and basic covariates. Its frozen primary analysis asks whether two resting high-frequency-oscillation (HFO) summaries plus age and stimulation settings predict a subject-level tapping-rate summary. That analysis must remain an immutable exploratory result. The literature below motivates a separate follow-on protocol that distinguishes resting neural traits, concurrent movement decoding, and movement-versus-rest neural modulation.

The reviewed studies are not treated as interchangeable replications. They differ in species, recording location, behavioral target, observation duration, and validation unit. In particular, thousands of temporal windows do not create thousands of independent participants. Any methodology transferred to this project must therefore be evaluated with complete subject-level separation and with preprocessing, feature reduction, and model selection fitted only on training subjects.

## Reviewed literature

### 1. Bore et al. (2020): prediction of mild parkinsonism from cortical and subthalamic oscillations

Bore and colleagues analyzed chronic local-field-potential recordings from a 12-channel subdural ECoG grid over primary motor cortex and a six-channel subthalamic nucleus (STN) array in a nonhuman-primate model measured before and after induction of mild parkinsonism. They represented the signals with power in conventional frequency bands and beta-to-gamma/HFO phase-amplitude coupling (PAC), then compared radial-basis-function support-vector machines and k-nearest-neighbor classifiers. Hyperparameters were optimized with Bayesian methods and performance was estimated with repeated ten-fold cross-validation. Parkinsonism was associated with increased beta, high-gamma, and HFO power; M1 HFO power was particularly discriminative, while high-beta power was most informative in STN. Fusing several spectral features generally outperformed individual features, whereas PAC alone contributed less. This study supports retaining HFO as a candidate signal but argues against compressing the entire spectrum into one 80–500 Hz power value. It also motivates a prespecified multiband feature block. Its within-animal state-classification design does not, however, demonstrate cross-person generalization and therefore cannot justify row-level or window-level random splitting in the present human cohort. [Bore et al., *Journal of Neurophysiology*, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00534.2020)

### 2. Muthuraman et al. (2021): frequency-specific networks and bradykinesia severity

Muthuraman and colleagues studied 20 people with PD receiving bilateral STN stimulation. Participants underwent 64-channel EEG, electromyography, and motion recording during rest, finger tapping, hand grasping, and pronation-supination at four individualized stimulation levels. The analysis separated low beta, high beta, low gamma, and high gamma activity, localized frequency-specific cortical networks, and used structural-equation modeling to relate changes in network activity across stimulation levels to changes in movement amplitude and speed. Motor and premotor cortical nodes were recurrently informative, but the relevant network and frequency depended on the movement and kinematic parameter. This work builds beyond single-band biomarker studies by showing that bradykinesia is not represented by one universal oscillatory measure. For CURVE-ICON-DBSO, it motivates separating stimulation condition, movement type, spectral band, cortical region, and kinematic outcome instead of asking one global HFO average to explain all tapping behavior. It also supports examining speed, amplitude, and variability in addition to rate when those targets can be derived reliably from the glove. [Muthuraman et al., *NeuroImage: Clinical*, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nicl.2021.102857)

### 3. Köhler et al. (2022): beta–gamma coupling during repetitive movement

Köhler and colleagues recorded high-density EEG from people with PD and control participants at rest and during repetitive finger pressing and tapping. Their kinematic outcomes included tapping rate, inter-tap variability, completion ratio, and change over the course of a trial. After artifact rejection and source localization, they measured beta-phase to broadband-gamma-amplitude coupling in premotor, primary motor, and somatosensory regions. Although resting and pressing conditions showed group differences in PAC, the repetitive tapping tasks did not show a simple PAC difference despite slower behavior in PD. This negative result is important for the present project: PAC is physiologically motivated but should not be assumed to be a superior feature or added without adequate artifact, waveform-shape, and surrogate controls. The study also advances the target design beyond dominant frequency alone. A meaningful transfer would derive rate, variability, amplitude/velocity, and sequence-effect measures from the glove and test them separately, rather than combining them after observing results. [Köhler et al., *Journal of Neurophysiology*, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00541.2021)

### 4. Merk et al. (2022): ECoG grip-force decoding in Parkinson's disease

Merk and colleagues recorded intraoperative sensorimotor ECoG and STN local-field potentials from 11 people with PD while they performed a grip-force task. Their real-time-compatible pipeline generated power features in eight frequency bands and concatenated up to 500 ms of preceding neural context. They compared Elastic Net, linear Wiener filters, neural networks, source-power comodulation, and XGBoost, with hyperparameter selection inside nested cross-validation. XGBoost produced the strongest ECoG force decoding, while Wiener filters were competitive for STN data; ECoG generally outperformed STN, and adding channels did not automatically improve performance. This is the closest published methodology-transfer template for the synchronized ECoG and glove streams in CURVE-ICON-DBSO. It suggests that the main follow-on target should be continuous or window-level glove movement, evaluated with a lagged regularized linear decoder before nonlinear boosting. The present project must be more conservative about generalization: all temporal windows from an outer-test subject must remain held out together, and model comparisons must not select the best channel or lag on the full cohort. [Merk et al., *eLife*, 2022](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.75126)

### 5. Lauro et al. (2023): distinct decoding signatures for tremor and bradykinesia

Lauro and colleagues combined quantitative motor behavior with STN and cortical recordings from people with PD. Behavioral data were divided into seven-second epochs describing tremor, slowness, and effective motor control. Linear support-vector regression used 42 spectral features spanning 3–400 Hz, with canonical bands divided into finer sub-bands; shuffled signal–behavior correspondences supplied null models. Cortical decoding generally outperformed subcortical decoding, and tremor and slowness exhibited nearly opposing spectral weights, indicating that separate symptoms should not be collapsed into one motor-severity target. This work builds on multiband studies by retaining an interpretable linear decoder while substantially increasing spectral resolution. It motivates linear SVR as a serious comparator in CURVE-ICON-DBSO and supports separate target-specific models. Its temporal cross-validation occurred within task sessions, so the present project should test the harder question of whether the representation generalizes to unseen subjects rather than directly comparing headline performance values. [Lauro et al., *eLife*, 2023](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.84135)

### 6. Köhler et al. (2024): temporal dynamics of volitional action under dopamine and DBS

Köhler and colleagues analyzed ECoG and STN recordings from 25 people with PD performing self-initiated upper-limb movements under OFF-therapy, dopaminergic-medication, and STN-DBS conditions. ECoG and STN signals were decomposed into seven bands from theta through 201–400 Hz at 100 ms temporal resolution. Linear discriminant analysis separated premovement neural activity from rest, while readiness potentials and Granger-causality analyses quantified timing and directional cortico-subthalamic communication. ECoG classified movement intention above chance and more accurately than STN signals; dopamine and DBS shortened the inferred intention-to-execution interval and shifted coupling from beta toward theta. This study builds on static spectral decoding by showing that when a feature occurs may be as important as its average magnitude. For the present data, it motivates event- or phase-aligned analysis, lagged features, and explicit comparison of premovement, movement, and rest windows. It also warns against treating stimulation or medication state merely as ordinary predictive columns when they may alter the neural representation itself. [Köhler et al., *Brain*, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awae219)

### 7. Guarin et al. (2024): richer finger-tapping phenotypes from video

Guarin and colleagues analyzed finger-tapping videos from 66 people with PD and 24 age-matched controls. Hand-pose estimation produced movement trajectories from which the investigators derived conventional measures such as amplitude, velocity, and amplitude decay, as well as nonlinear or severity-specific characteristics. They compared multiclass, ordinal-binary, and tiered classification strategies and found that different kinematic features became informative at different levels of motor severity. Although this study did not use ECoG, it is directly relevant to the behavioral side of CURVE-ICON-DBSO. It demonstrates that a single dominant-frequency summary discards clinically meaningful variation in speed, decrement, amplitude, and regularity. Building on the PAC study's multidimensional kinematics, it supports a separately validated glove feature set containing tapping rate, interval variability, movement amplitude or velocity, and sequence effect. These measures should be derived without selecting whichever one correlates best with the neural data. [Guarin et al., *IEEE Transactions on Neural Systems and Rehabilitation Engineering*, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/TNSRE.2024.3416446)

### 8. Fast, accurate, and interpretable ECoG decoding with dynamic mode decomposition (2024)

This study evaluated dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) using multichannel ECoG recorded from 11 patients during grasping, hand opening, and pinching. Five-hundred-millisecond post-cue windows were decomposed into spatial dynamic modes, which were transformed into real-valued spatial-node and spatial-edge features. Regularized linear support-vector machines classified movement types, with DMD rank and model settings selected inside nested cross-validation. Spatial-node DMD features were strongly related to 80–200 Hz activity yet were more reproducible across trials than conventional power features, and they outperformed high-gamma power for movement classification. This work proposes a representation-level improvement rather than another nonlinear prediction model. It offers the most defensible novel-method experiment for CURVE-ICON-DBSO: compare standard multiband power with DMD node features under identical subject-held-out folds. A successful result would suggest that stable multichannel dynamics contain information missed by subject-level power averages; a null result would still provide a direct cross-subject test of a recent ECoG representation. [*Communications Biology*, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-024-06294-3)

### 9. Generalizable invasive neural decoding and connectomics (2025)

The 2025 py_neuromodulation study assembled ECoG recordings from 56 implanted participants across several cohorts, including 38 people with PD, and evaluated rest-versus-movement decoding without patient-specific training. The pipeline standardized streaming-compatible preprocessing and extracted multiband spectral, burst, waveform-shape, aperiodic, and connectivity-related features. It also explored anatomical normalization and connectomic channel selection so that models could operate on common spatial locations rather than patient-specific channel numbers. Cross-participant movement detection was above chance across participants, with the strongest performance over sensorimotor cortex; theta, high-beta, and high-gamma information contributed prominently. This study builds on earlier single-center and patient-specific decoders by making cross-patient generalization the central question. It provides a direct design precedent for CURVE-ICON-DBSO's outer subject holdout, region-aware channel representation, and feature-block ablations. The current cohort is much smaller, so only a restricted, prespecified subset of these features should be tested. [*Nature Biomedical Engineering*, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41551-025-01467-9)

### 10. Multitarget brain implants for generalized symptom decoding (recent preprint)

This recent preprint analyzed approximately 581 hours of chronic cortical and subcortical recordings from 16 people with PD alongside wearable estimates of bradykinesia, tremor, and dyskinesia. It compared CatBoost, XGBoost, random forests, linear models, PCA followed by linear models, and the CEBRA contrastive-learning framework. Feature families included raw amplitude, line length, Hjorth parameters, bursts, waveform sharpness, Fourier/Welch power, and aperiodic parameters. Models were evaluated with leave-one-hemisphere-out and leave-one-subject-out procedures; combined cortical and subcortical features improved symptom decoding over single beta-band feedback. This work extends the feature-rich, subject-generalized direction of the 2025 study into naturalistic chronic data and representation learning. It is relevant as an upper bound on what may become possible with long recordings, not as evidence that deep learning is appropriate for 31 subject-level rows. In CURVE-ICON-DBSO, CEBRA should be optional and secondary, trained only on window-level data within each outer training fold and compared against simpler representations. The source is a preprint and its findings should be described accordingly until peer review is confirmed. [Preprint full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13193127/)

## Synthesis and proposed work with the CURVE-ICON-DBSO data

The literature collectively suggests that the original resting-HFO hypothesis is scientifically reasonable but under-represented by two recording-wide summaries. Across studies, informative neural representations are multiband, region-aware, and temporally resolved; absolute power, movement-related modulation, burst dynamics, aperiodic structure, and coupling can contain complementary information. I therefore propose retaining the corrected five-feature resting analysis as the frozen primary exploratory result and registering a distinct follow-on analysis with three arms: REST features predicting subject-level tapping phenotype, concurrent MOVE ECoG predicting time-resolved glove movement, and MOVE-minus-REST neural modulation predicting subject-level tapping phenotype. The central scientific comparison is not which algorithm wins, but whether neural information is a stable resting trait, a concurrent state signal, or a state-change signal.

For the subject-level arms, I propose a restricted feature dictionary containing anatomically summarized relative or aperiodic-adjusted theta, alpha, low-beta, high-beta, low-gamma, high-gamma, and HFO power; beta-burst occupancy and duration; Hjorth mobility and complexity; line length; spectral entropy; and movement-minus-rest log-power changes. Robustly derived glove outcomes should include the frozen tapping-rate target plus separately declared secondary outcomes for interval variability, velocity or movement amplitude, and sequence effect. Ridge regression should be the primary expanded-feature model, with linear SVR, Bayesian Ridge, one conservative Gaussian-process model, and one shallow boosted-tree model as prespecified comparators. Feature extraction, imputation, scaling, dimensionality reduction, and hyperparameter choice must occur inside nested subject-grouped validation. Model and feature-block comparisons should use the same outer folds and corrected subject-level or autocorrelation-preserving permutation nulls.

For the methodology-transfer arm, I propose constructing 500 ms to 1 s ECoG windows with 100–250 ms updates and up to 500 ms of preceding neural context, then predicting continuous glove velocity or tapping-vigor envelopes. A lagged Ridge/Wiener decoder should establish the linear temporal baseline, followed by linear SVR and one shallow XGBoost or CatBoost implementation. Standard multiband features should then be compared with spatial-node DMD features as the main novel representation experiment. Metrics should be computed within each held-out subject and summarized across subjects, never pooled as if windows were independent. Circular time shifts or blockwise target permutations should preserve temporal autocorrelation. If concurrent decoding succeeds while resting prediction remains null, the appropriate conclusion will be successful transfer of movement-state decoding but no evidence for a resting trait biomarker. If movement-minus-rest or DMD features improve subject-held-out prediction, that will motivate a new state-change or dynamical representation hypothesis for external confirmation.

## Post-study cross-reference (2026-11-16)

The three-arm protocol motivated by this review (§Synthesis) was executed under the frozen contract v1.0.0 and is reported in `docs/results_followon_real.md`. In brief: the transferred time-resolved decoding methodology (subject-held-out folds, lagged linear decoders, autocorrelation-preserving nulls) detected a tiny above-null signal (median r ≈ .06, shift-null p = .001) that falls far short of the naive temporal baselines (persistence r = .92) — transfer was NOT successful under the prespecified definition — and the DMD representation (§8) did not outperform conventional band power on held-out subjects, mirroring the direction of §8's own caution. The resting-trait and state-change arms were global nulls. These results do not validate the reviewed methodologies at this cohort scale and strengthen the review's repeated warnings about cross-subject generalization at pilot n.
